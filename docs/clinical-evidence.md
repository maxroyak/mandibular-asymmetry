## 1. Ramus Height Measurement

### Method Name
**Ramus Height (Posterior Mandibular Height)** — the most widely used vertical measurement for mandibular asymmetry assessment on panoramic radiographs.

### Landmarks Used

| Landmark | Abbreviation | Definition |
|----------|-------------|------------|
| **Condylion** | Co (or CoR/CoL) | Most superior point of the condylar head |
| **Gonion** | Go (or GoR/GoL) | Most posterior-inferior point at the angle of the mandible (intersection of posterior border and inferior border) |

### Distance Measured
**Co–Go** (Condylion to Gonion) — the linear vertical distance from the most superior point of the condylar head to gonion. Also referred to as **posterior mandibular height (PMH)** by Laster et al. (2005).

Some studies measure this as a single distance (Co–Go). Others decompose it into:
- **Condylar height** (Co to sigmoid notch)
- **Ramus height proper** (sigmoid notch to Go)
- **Total ramus height** = condylar height + ramus height = Co–Go

### Reproducibility on OPG

**Moderate evidence supports OPG reliability for ramus height:**

- **Ongkosuwito et al. (2009)** [PMID 19254052] found that the OPG (orthopantomogram) had comparable reliability to lateral cephalograms for measuring Co–Go, Go–Me (menton), and Co–Me. Intraobserver ICC was good to excellent. No significant left-right magnification difference was detected.

- **Faryal & Shaikh (2022)** [PMID 36550653] found no statistically significant difference between OPG and lateral cephalogram for ramus height measurements (p=0.226 right, p=0.177 left), supporting the use of OPG for ramus height.

- **Stăncioiu et al. (2025)** [PMID 40004826] found strong positive correlations between OPG and lateral cephalogram for ramus height measurements (rs range: 0.800–0.946; p < 0.001).

- **Bal et al. (2018)** [PMID 29500898] reported ICC for ramus height measurement, with mean ramus asymmetry of 2.90% ± 2.58% in a normal young population (n=776).

### Known Limitations

1. **Condylion identification is challenging** — the most superior point of the condylar head is difficult to identify consistently, especially when the condyle is flattened, resorbed, or has an irregular shape (see §7).
2. **Gonion identification requires construction** — on panoramic images, gonion is typically constructed as the intersection of the posterior border tangent and the inferior border tangent, which introduces variability.
3. **Vertical magnification** — panoramic radiographs have inherent vertical magnification (typically 1.24–1.37×, Scarfe et al. 1998), which is relatively consistent bilaterally but must be considered for absolute measurements.
4. **Positioning sensitivity** — head displacement from the focal trough affects measurement accuracy (Laster et al. 2005).
5. **2D projection** — a 3D structure is projected onto a 2D image; medial-lateral condylar position differences are not captured.

### Evidence Quality: **MODERATE**
Multiple studies confirm acceptable reliability for vertical ramus height on OPG, with comparable performance to lateral cephalograms. However, landmark identification (especially condylion) remains a source of error.

### Key References
- Habets et al. (1988) — J Oral Rehabil 15(5): 465–471 [PMID 3244055]
- Laster et al. (2005) — Dentomaxillofac Radiol 34: 346–350 [PMID 16227476]
- Ongkosuwito et al. (2009) — Cleft Palate Craniofac J 46: 147–153 [PMID 19254052]
- Faryal & Shaikh (2022) — J Ayub Med Coll Abbottabad 34(S4): 172–175 [PMID 36550653]

## 2. Condylar Height

### Method Name
**Condylar Height (CH)** — measured as a component of total ramus height in the Habets method.

### Landmarks Used

| Landmark | Definition |
|----------|------------|
| **Condylion (Co)** | Most superior point of the condylar head |
| **Sigmoid notch (Sn)** / **Incisura mandibulae** | Deepest point of the sigmoid (mandibular) notch — the concave point between the condylar and coronoid processes |
| **Gonion (Go)** | Most posterior-inferior point at the mandibular angle |

### Distances Measured

| Measurement | Definition |
|-------------|------------|
| **Condylar height (CH)** | Co → Sn (most superior condyle point to deepest sigmoid notch point) |
| **Ramus height (RH)** | Sn → Go (sigmoid notch to gonion) |
| **Total height (TH)** | Co → Go (condylion to gonion) = CH + RH |

The Habets method (1988) explicitly separates condylar height from ramus height, measuring both independently. The asymmetry index is then calculated separately for condylar, ramal, and total height.

### Relationship to Ramus Height
- Condylar height is a **component** of total ramus height (Co–Go).
- Condylar asymmetry is consistently reported as **more prevalent and more severe** than ramus or total height asymmetry across multiple studies.
- **Sop et al. (2016)** [PMID 27077261]: "Asymmetries in general were not rare and were more present in the condylar height rather than in the height of the ramus." Median condylar asymmetry index was 7.3% in Class II/2 subjects.
- **Pinto-Wong & Arriola-Guillén (2024)** [PMID 39670032]: Condylar asymmetry present in 81.4% of adults, ramus asymmetry in 48.6%, using a 3% threshold.
- **Saglam & Sanli (2004)** [PMID 15318257]: Mean condylar asymmetry of 11.11% ± 11.03% in TMD patients vs 8.36% ± 6.27% in controls.

### Limitations
1. **Sigmoid notch identification** — the deepest point of the sigmoid notch can be difficult to identify on OPG, especially when the notch is shallow or the coronoid process overlaps the condylar region.
2. **Condylar height is more variable** — the condyle is subject to remodeling, resorption, hypertrophy, and morphological variation, making condylar height inherently more variable than ramus height.
3. **Higher asymmetry in normal populations** — condylar asymmetry is found in 60–80% of asymptomatic individuals, raising questions about its diagnostic specificity.

### Evidence Quality: **MODERATE**
The separation of condylar from ramal height is well-established in the Habets tradition, but sigmoid notch identification adds an additional landmark identification error.

### Key References
- Habets et al. (1988) — J Oral Rehabil 15(5): 465–471 [PMID 3244055]
- Türker & Öztürk Yaşar (2022) — BMC Oral Health 22: 216 [PMID 35941596]
- Pinto-Wong & Arriola-Guillén (2024) — J Clin Exp Dent 16(11): e881–e887 [PMID 39670032]
- Alanazi et al. (2026) — Cureus 18(1): e101844 [PMID 41717179]

## 3. Mandibular Body Length

### Method Name
**Mandibular Body Length** — horizontal measurement from gonion to a midline reference point.

### Landmarks Used

| Landmark | Abbreviation | Definition |
|----------|-------------|------------|
| **Gonion** | Go (GoR/GoL) | Most posterior-inferior point at the mandibular angle |
| **Menton** | Me | Most inferior point of the mandibular symphysis |
| **Symphysis midpoint** | — | Midpoint of the mandibular symphysis (alternative to Me) |

### Distance Measured
- **Go–Me**: Linear distance from gonion to menton (most commonly used).
- **Go–Symphysis midpoint**: Alternative when menton is difficult to identify.

For bilateral comparison: **GoR–Me** (right body length) vs **GoL–Me** (left body length), where Me is a shared midline landmark.

### Reproducibility
- **Ongkosuwito et al. (2009)** [PMID 19254052]: Go–Me on OPG showed comparable reliability to lateral cephalogram.
- **Faryal & Shaikh (2022)** [PMID 36550653]: Statistically significant differences between OPG and lateral cephalogram for mandibular body length (p=0.000) and total mandibular length (p=0.000). **OPG is less reliable for body length than for ramus height.**
- **Stăncioiu et al. (2025)** [PMID 40004826]: "The mandibular body length showed weaker correlations between the two methods" compared to ramus height and gonial angle.

### Symmetrical Landmark Selection for Right/Left Comparison
- **Menton (Me)** is a single midline landmark shared by both sides, making it the natural reference for body length comparison.
- The right and left gonion (GoR, GoL) must be identified symmetrically on both sides — any asymmetry in gonion placement directly affects the body length comparison.
- **Silvestrini-Biavati et al. (2014)** [PMID 24992843] extended the Habets method to include mandibular body length measurement with an asymmetry index, finding significant differences in body length asymmetry between crossbite and non-crossbite groups (index 2.3% vs 1.4%, p=0.04).

### Critical Limitation
**Horizontal measurements on OPG are significantly less reliable than vertical measurements** (see §6). The panoramic image geometry causes variable horizontal magnification across the image, making side-to-side horizontal distance comparisons inherently less accurate than vertical comparisons. Multiple studies caution against relying on horizontal OPG measurements for asymmetry assessment.

### Evidence Quality: **LOW TO MODERATE**
Ramus height is well-supported; body length on OPG has weaker evidence due to horizontal distortion. Studies explicitly note weaker correlation for body length vs ramus height when comparing OPG to cephalograms.

### Key References
- Ongkosuwito et al. (2009) — Cleft Palate Craniofac J 46: 147–153 [PMID 19254052]
- Faryal & Shaikh (2022) — J Ayub Med Coll Abbottabad 34(S4): 172–175 [PMID 36550653]
- Stăncioiu et al. (2025) — J Clin Med [PMID 40004826]
- Silvestrini-Biavati et al. (2014) — Indian J Dent Res 25(2): 167–172 [PMID 24992843]

## 4. Habets Asymmetry Index

### Method Name
**Habets Asymmetry Index** (also referred to as the Habets method, LRHR method, or vertical asymmetry index)

### Original Publication
**Habets LLM, Bezuur JN, Naeiji M, Hansson TL.** "The Orthopantomogram, an aid in diagnosis of temporomandibular joint problems. II. The vertical symmetry." *Journal of Oral Rehabilitation* 1988; 15(5): 465–471. [PMID 3244055]

### Landmarks Used
- **Condylion (Co)** — most superior point of condylar head
- **Sigmoid notch (Sn)** — deepest point of the mandibular notch
- **Gonion (Go)** — most posterior-inferior point of mandibular angle

### Measurements
The Habets method measures three bilateral vertical distances:
1. **Condylar height** (Co–Sn): R and L
2. **Ramus height** (Sn–Go): R and L
3. **Total height** (Co–Go): R and L

### Formula

```
Asymmetry Index (AI) = (R − L) / (R + L) × 100
```

Where:
- R = right side measurement
- L = left side measurement
- The result is a **signed percentage** — positive indicates right dominance, negative indicates left dominance

Three separate indices are calculated:
- **Condylar Asymmetry Index (CAI)** = (R_CoSn − L_CoSn) / (R_CoSn + L_CoSn) × 100
- **Ramal Asymmetry Index (RAI)** = (R_SnGo − L_SnGo) / (R_SnGo + L_SnGo) × 100
- **Total Asymmetry Index (TAI)** = (R_CoGo − L_CoGo) / (R_CoGo + L_CoGo) × 100

### Validation Studies

| Study | Year | Sample | Key Finding |
|-------|------|--------|-------------|
| Habets et al. | 1988 | 152 patients | Defined the method; found significant difference between routine dental and CMD patients for condylar height symmetry |
| Bezuur et al. | 1988 | 31 CMD patients | 74% of CMD patients had >3% condylar asymmetry [PMID 3236126] |
| Bezuur et al. | 1989 | 43 CMD patients | 67% had >3% vertical condylar asymmetry; myogenous patients showed greater asymmetry (9.10%) than arthrogenous (4.83%) [PMID 2746413] |
| Türp et al. | 1995 | 25 dry skulls | **Critical validation**: sensitivity 0.8 required specificity of only 0.20–0.25, resulting in a very high false positive rate. Questioned the method's diagnostic utility. [PMID 7610365] |
| Saglam & Sanli | 2004 | 50 patients | Mean CAI 11.11% in TMD, 8.36% in controls; no significant difference [PMID 15318257] |
| Halicioglu et al. | 2013 | 55 subjects | CAI values significantly high vs 3% threshold in both groups; no between-group difference [PMID 23001188] |
| Silvestrini-Biavati et al. | 2014 | 28 subjects | Extended Habets with horizontal/diagonal measurements; found significant differences in condylar and total height between crossbite and non-crossbite groups [PMID 24992843] |
| Sop et al. | 2016 | 205 children | Condylar asymmetry more prevalent than ramus; Class II/2 most affected [PMID 27077261] |
| Bal et al. | 2018 | 776 subjects | Mean ramus asymmetry 2.90% ± 2.58%; 10.8% prevalence above threshold [PMID 29500898] |
| Alkis & Pekince | 2023 | 100 patients | Asymmetry indices reliably obtained from both OPG and PA cephalometric images [PMID 36635560] |
| Pinto-Wong & Arriola-Guillén | 2024 | 210 adults | 39.5% mandibular, 81.4% condylar, 48.6% ramus asymmetry using 3% threshold [PMID 39670032] |
| Paduano et al. | 2025 | 48 TMD patients | Used Habets method to track changes after occlusal splint therapy; found improvement in condylar asymmetry [PMID 40277506] |

### Limitations

1. **Low diagnostic specificity** — Türp et al. (1995) demonstrated on dry skulls that achieving 80% sensitivity required accepting specificity of only 0.20–0.25 for ramus height and 0.20 for condylar height, resulting in a very high rate of false positive diagnoses.
2. **High prevalence in normal populations** — condylar asymmetry >3% is found in 60–80% of asymptomatic individuals, questioning the 3% threshold's clinical significance.
3. **Technical error contribution** — Habets et al. (1987) [PMID 3478455] showed that up to 6% of side-to-side difference may be attributable to technical/positioning factors alone.
4. **2D projection limitation** — the method measures projected vertical distances, not true 3D anatomy.
5. **No universally validated threshold** — the 3% threshold is widely used but not clinically validated (see §8).
6. **Original formula produces a signed value** — interpretation requires understanding direction (which side is larger).

### Alternative Asymmetry Indices

#### Kjellberg Asymmetry Index
- **Badel et al. (2023)** [PMID 37427358] and **Sofyanti et al. (2018)** [PMID 29946446] reference a "Kjellberg technique" for vertical mandibular asymmetry.
- The Kjellberg index expresses symmetry as a **percentage of the smaller relative to the larger side**: (smaller / larger) × 100, where 100% = perfect symmetry and lower values = greater asymmetry.
- This provides a different numerical scale than Habets: values closer to 100% indicate symmetry, while lower values indicate asymmetry.
- Less commonly used than Habets in the literature.

#### Relative Difference (see §5)
- A variant expressing the percentage difference relative to the mean rather than the sum.

#### Simple Percentage Difference
- (|R − L| / R) × 100 or (|R − L| / max(R,L)) × 100
- Less standardized; used in some clinical contexts but lacks the literature support of the Habets formula.

### Evidence Quality: **MODERATE**
The Habets index is the most widely used and cited method for vertical mandibular asymmetry assessment on OPG, with over 35 years of literature. However, its diagnostic specificity has been questioned (Türp 1995), and no validated clinical threshold exists. It is best used as a **screening/comparative tool**, not a diagnostic standard.

### Key References
- Habets et al. (1988) — J Oral Rehabil 15(5): 465–471 [PMID 3244055]
- Habets et al. (1987) — J Oral Rehabil 14(5): 465–471 [PMID 3478455] (vertical magnification)
- Türp et al. (1995) — Schweiz Monatsschr Zahnmed 105: 755–760 [PMID 7610365]

## 5. Relative Difference Formula

### Method Name
**Relative Difference (Percentage Difference from Mean)**

### Formula

```
Relative Difference (%) = |R − L| / ((R + L) / 2) × 100
```

Which simplifies to:

```
Relative Difference (%) = 2 × |R − L| / (R + L) × 100
```

### Relationship to Habets Index

The relative difference formula is mathematically related to the Habets asymmetry index:

| Formula | Expression |
|---------|-----------|
| Habets AI | (R − L) / (R + L) × 100 |
| Absolute Habets AI | \|R − L\| / (R + L) × 100 |
| **Relative Difference** | **\|R − L\| / ((R + L) / 2) × 100 = 2 × \|Habets AI\|** |

The relative difference is exactly **twice** the absolute value of the Habets asymmetry index. For example:
- If Habets AI = 3%, then Relative Difference = 6%
- If Habets AI = 5%, then Relative Difference = 10%

### When This Is Used vs the Habets Index

- The **Habets index** is used in the research literature and in most published studies on mandibular asymmetry. It produces a signed value (positive = right larger, negative = left larger).
- The **relative difference** formula is more intuitive for clinical communication: it expresses how much one side differs from the bilateral average, similar to how percentage change is commonly understood.
- The relative difference is well-suited for a clinical decision-support tool because:
  - It uses an absolute value (always positive), making it easier to display
  - The value represents the percentage deviation from the mean of both sides
  - It avoids the potential confusion of signed asymmetry indices

### Scientific Basis
The relative difference formula is a standard mathematical expression for percentage deviation from the mean of two values. It is not a separately published clinical method but is derived from basic statistical principles. Its mathematical equivalence to twice the absolute Habets index means that all validation studies of the Habets index apply equally, with appropriate scaling.

**Important note for implementation:** If the Habets 3% threshold is used, the equivalent relative difference threshold is 6%. The application must be clear about which formula is being displayed to avoid confusion.

### Evidence Quality: **MODERATE** (by derivation from Habets)
Not independently validated, but mathematically equivalent to 2 × |Habets AI|.

### Key References
- Derived from Habets et al. (1988) [PMID 3244055]
- Standard statistical methodology

## 6. Magnification and Distortion

### How Panoramic Radiograph Geometry Affects Measurements

Panoramic radiography uses a rotating X-ray source and detector that creates a curved focal trough (imaging layer). Objects within the focal trough are relatively sharp; objects outside are blurred and distorted. The geometry produces:

1. **Non-uniform magnification** — magnification varies across the image depending on object position relative to the rotation center and focal trough.
2. **Different vertical and horizontal magnification** — vertical and horizontal magnification factors differ, and horizontal magnification is more variable.
3. **Distortion** — the ratio of vertical to horizontal magnification (distortion index) varies across the image, causing shape distortion.

### Vertical vs Horizontal Measurement Reliability

**Vertical measurements are more reliable than horizontal measurements on OPG.** This is a consistent finding across multiple studies:

| Study | Year | Finding |
|-------|------|---------|
| Scarfe et al. [PMID 9482025] | 1998 | Vertical magnification: 1.24–1.37× (linear, predictable). Horizontal magnification: 1.01–1.63× (highly variable). Distortion index: 0.80–0.89. |
| Habets et al. [PMID 3478455] | 1987 | Within 10mm of centered position, vertical side-to-side differences <6%. |
| Schulze et al. [PMID 10900960] | 2000 | Horizontal rotation of skull affects magnification factors (VF) in all mandibular regions (p=0.0001); premolar region showed up to 10.3% variation. Vertical inclinations affect horizontal VF only in premolar region. |
| Devlin & Yuan [PMID 22933529] | 2013 | Vertical magnification SD: 0.0067–0.0018; horizontal magnification SD: 0.0445–0.0068. **Vertical magnification is more consistent than horizontal.** |
| Laster et al. [PMID 16227476] | 2005 | "Greatest differences were noted for horizontal measurements and shifted skull positions." Vertical measurements (PMH, condyle-to-sigmoid notch) performed better than horizontal (G-MF, 20mm wire). |
| Ladeira et al. [PMID 22282504] | 2012 | Both horizontal and vertical measurements show magnification; differences between regions were significant (p<0.05). |
| Martins et al. [PMID 34757830] | 2022 | Mapped focal trough: all layers curved (39°–51°), varying in position and thickness. Mandibular layers thicker than maxillary. |

**Key conclusion:** Vertical measurements (ramus height, condylar height) are suitable for OPG-based asymmetry assessment. Horizontal measurements (body length, intergonial distance) are less reliable and should be interpreted with caution.

### Why Horizontal Measurements Are Less Reliable

1. **Rotation geometry** — the X-ray beam rotates around the patient's head, and the horizontal magnification depends on the distance from the rotation center to the object. This distance varies significantly across the mandibular arch.
2. **Focal trough curvature** — the focal trough is curved, and objects at different horizontal positions are at different distances from the rotation center, causing variable horizontal magnification.
3. **Object depth sensitivity** — horizontal magnification is highly sensitive to the medial-lateral position of the object within the focal trough. A small medial-lateral displacement causes significant horizontal magnification change.
4. **Beam angle variation** — the X-ray beam projection angle varies along the panoramic image, causing different horizontal distortion at different locations.

### Effect of Head Rotation on Side-to-Side Comparison

**Head rotation (yaw) is the most problematic positioning error for asymmetry assessment:**

- **Schulze et al. (2000)** [PMID 10900960]: When the skull is rotated within the horizontal plane, magnification factors are significantly influenced in **all anatomical regions** of the mandible (p=0.0001). This is the most critical positioning error because it asymmetrically changes the magnification of right vs left sides.
- **Laster et al. (2005)** [PMID 16227476]: Rotated skull positions still allowed 70% accuracy for detecting asymmetry (compared to 67% for ideal and 47% for shifted). Internal calibration reference improved accuracy.
- **Ongkosuwito et al. (2009)** [PMID 19254052]: No significant differences were found in the magnification factor of the left and right side of the mandible — but this was under controlled skull positioning.

**Clinical implication:** If a patient's head is rotated during the OPG exposure, one side of the mandible may be magnified more than the other, creating an **apparent asymmetry** that does not reflect true anatomy. The application cannot correct for this from a single 2D image.

### Effect of Head Tilt

- Head tilt (roll, lateral flexion) affects the vertical orientation of structures and can introduce apparent vertical asymmetry.
- **Schulze et al. (2000)** [PMID 10900960]: Vertical inclinations of the skull affected horizontal magnification factor only in the premolar region (p=0.0069), suggesting that head tilt has a more localized effect than head rotation.
- **Habets et al. (1987)** [PMID 3478455]: Showed that vertical magnification changes with horizontal displacement but remains relatively consistent bilaterally for small (<10mm) displacements.

### Calibration Approach

- **Laster et al. (2005)** [PMID 16227476]: "Use of an arbitrary magnification correction was consistently less accurate than use of an internal calibration and resulted in general underestimation of actual dimensions." **Internal calibration (using a known reference visible on the image) is strongly preferred over using the manufacturer's stated magnification factor.**
- For relative measurements (asymmetry indices), the calibration factor cancels out in the ratio, making the Habets formula inherently more robust to magnification errors than absolute measurements — **provided the magnification is symmetric** (which it may not be if the head is rotated).

### Evidence Quality: **HIGH**
Multiple well-designed phantom and dry skull studies provide strong evidence on magnification and distortion characteristics of panoramic radiography.

## 7. Landmark Reproducibility

### Which Landmarks Are Most Reproducible on OPG

| Landmark | Reproducibility | Notes |
|----------|----------------|-------|
| **Menton (Me)** | Moderate–High | Midline structure; single point; generally well-defined on OPG. Intra-examiner reliability 0.96, inter-examiner 0.90 for mandibular height measurements involving Me (Pan et al. 2009 [PMID 19221572]). |
| **Gonion (Go)** | Moderate | Constructed landmark (intersection of posterior and inferior border tangents). Subject to border identification variability. Listed as showing "poor agreement" between surgeons by Miloro et al. (2014) [PMID 24055177], but more reliable on OPG than on lateral cephalogram due to bilateral visualization. |
| **Condylion (Co)** | **Low–Moderate** | **Most problematic landmark.** Most superior point of condylar head; difficult to identify when condyle is flattened, resorbed, or irregular. Listed among "least reliable landmarks" by Durão et al. (2015) [PMID 26730368] and Miloro et al. (2014) [PMID 24055177]. Farther from best estimate in 2D than 3D by Chien et al. (2009) [PMID 19474253]. |
| **Sigmoid notch (Sn)** | Low–Moderate | Deepest point of mandibular notch; can be obscured by coronoid process overlap. Farther from best estimate in 2D than 3D (Chien et al. 2009). |

### Which Landmarks Are Problematic

**Condylion (Co)** is consistently identified as one of the least reproducible landmarks:

- **Durão et al. (2015)** [PMID 26730368]: Condylion was among the least reliable landmarks for both orthodontists and radiologists. The Co–Gn plane was the least consistent measurement (SD 4.43 mm).
- **Miloro et al. (2014)** [PMID 24055177]: "Porion, condylion, and gonion showed poor agreement and reliability between examiners" regardless of experience level.
- **Chien et al. (2009)** [PMID 19474253]: Condylion and sigmoid notch were farther from best estimate in 2D than in 3D CBCT, indicating that 2D imaging (including OPG) makes these landmarks harder to identify.

### Condylion Identification Challenges

1. **Anatomical variation** — condylar head shape varies widely (round, oval, flattened, resorbed, hypertrophic). The "most superior point" is ambiguous on irregular condyles.
2. **Superimposition** — the condylar head may be superimposed by surrounding structures (zygomatic arch, external auditory canal).
3. **Image quality** — the condylar region is at the edge of the focal trough and may be less sharp than the dental region.
4. **2D projection** — a 3D condylar head is projected onto a 2D image; the "most superior point" depends on the condyle's medio-lateral orientation, which varies between individuals.
5. **Remodeling and pathology** — condylar resorption (e.g., in JIA, TMD) changes the condylar shape, making the "most superior point" a moving target.

### Inter-examiner and Intra-examiner Reliability

| Study | Measurement | Intra-examiner ICC | Inter-examiner ICC |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Pan et al. (2009) [PMID 19221572] | Mandibular bone height (OPG) | 0.96 | 0.90 |
| Ongkosuwito et al. (2009) [PMID 19254052] | Co-Go, Go-Me, Co-Me (OPG) | Good to excellent (kappa) | Significant differences between orthodontists and surgeons |
| Mathew et al. (2011) [PMID 22114595] | Condylar changes (OPG) | High | High |
| Durão et al. (2015) [PMID 26730368] | Cephalometric landmarks | Excellent | Good |
| Popa et al. (2025) [PMID 41303135] | Ramus heights (OPG, AI-assisted) | ICC reported as high | High |

**General pattern:** Intra-examiner reliability is consistently higher than inter-examiner reliability. The same observer measuring the same image twice produces more consistent results than two different observers. This supports a workflow where the same clinician places all landmarks.

### Evidence Quality: **MODERATE**
Landmark reproducibility is well-studied for lateral cephalograms but less specifically for OPG. The condylion problem is consistently documented. Reliability figures from cephalometric studies likely overestimate OPG reliability for landmarks like condylion, as OPG provides less detailed condylar visualization.

## 8. Threshold Values

### Are There Validated Thresholds for "Clinically Significant" Asymmetry?

**No universally validated clinical threshold exists.** This is one of the most important findings of this review.

### The 3% Threshold

The most widely used threshold in the literature is **3%** for the Habets asymmetry index. This threshold originates from:

- **Bezuur et al. (1988)** [PMID 3236126]: Stated that "74% of the patients with CMD had more than the 3% of asymmetry regarded as within normal limits." This established 3% as the boundary of "normal" asymmetry.
- **Habets et al. (1987)** [PMID 3478455]: Showed that up to **6%** of side-to-side difference may be attributable to **technical/positioning factors** alone (vertical magnification within 10mm of centered position). The 3% threshold is therefore **below the technical error margin** identified by the original authors, making its use as a clinical cutoff questionable.

The 3% threshold has been adopted by numerous subsequent studies:
- Halicioglu et al. (2013) [PMID 23001188]: Used 3% threshold
- Abad-Santamaría et al. (2014) [PMID 24097050]: Used 3% threshold
- Pinto-Wong & Arriola-Guillén (2024) [PMID 39670032]: Used 3% threshold
- Silvestrini-Biavati et al. (2014) [PMID 24992843]: Referenced both 3% and 6% (twice the threshold)

### The 6% Technical Error Threshold

- **Habets et al. (1987)** [PMID 3478455] demonstrated that vertical magnification differences between sides could be up to 6% due to positioning alone (within 10mm of centered position).
- This suggests that asymmetry index values **below 6% may be entirely due to technical factors** and should not be considered clinically meaningful.
- Some authors interpret this as: **<3% = normal, 3–6% = borderline (possibly technical), >6% = likely true asymmetry**.

### Normal Variation

| Study | Population | Mean Asymmetry | Finding |
|-------|-----------|-----------------|---------|
| Bal et al. (2018) [PMID 29500898] | 776 young individuals (9–21y) | 2.90% ± 2.58% (ramus) | 10.8% had ramus asymmetry above threshold |
| Pinto-Wong & Arriola-Guillén (2024) [PMID 39670032] | 210 adults | — | 81.4% condylar, 48.6% ramus, 39.5% mandibular asymmetry above 3% |
| Saglam & Sanli (2004) [PMID 15318257] | 25 controls | 8.36% ± 6.27% (condylar) | No significant difference from TMD group |
| Abad-Santamaría et al. (2014) [PMID 24097050] | 20 normal occlusion | 6.93% ± 9.87% (condylar), 2.65% ± 1.90% (ramal) | CAI above 3% in normal group |

**The extremely high prevalence of "asymmetry" in normal populations when using the 3% threshold (81.4% condylar, 48.6% ramal) strongly suggests that 3% is too low to be clinically meaningful.**

### Severity Classification

Some studies have attempted severity classifications:

- **Sop et al. (2016)** [PMID 27077261]: Divided asymmetry into "mild," "moderate," and "severe" based on the asymmetry index distribution, with 64% of Class II/2 subjects showing "moderate and severe" condylar asymmetry.
- **Silvestrini-Biavati et al. (2014)** [PMID 24992843]: Used 3% as threshold and 6% as "twice the threshold" for more significant asymmetry.

However, no study has validated specific cutoff values against clinical outcomes (e.g., need for treatment, treatment success, progression of asymmetry).

### What Constitutes Asymmetry Requiring Further Investigation?

Based on the evidence:

| Asymmetry Index (Habets) | Relative Difference | Interpretation |
|--------------------------|--------------------|--------------------| 
| **< 3%** | < 6% | Within normal range; likely includes technical error |
| **3–6%** | 6–12% | Borderline; may be due to positioning/technical factors; monitor or correlate clinically |
| **> 6%** | > 12% | Likely true asymmetry; **recommend clinical correlation and 3D imaging (CBCT)** |

**This classification is based on expert opinion derived from the literature, not on validated clinical outcomes.** The application should present it as a guideline, not a diagnostic standard.

### Critical Caveat

**Türp et al. (1995)** [PMID 7610365] specifically tested the diagnostic accuracy of the Habets method on dry skulls and found that achieving 80% sensitivity required accepting specificity of only 0.20–0.25, "resulting in a very high rate of false positive diagnoses." This means that the asymmetry index is poor at distinguishing true asymmetry from measurement artifact, especially at low thresholds.

### Evidence Quality: **LOW** (for specific threshold values)
The 3% threshold is widely used but not clinically validated. The 6% technical error margin is better supported by experimental data. No validated clinical significance threshold exists.

## 9. Clinical Interpretation Guidance

### How Results Should Be Worded to Avoid Over-Diagnosis

Based on the PROJECT_CONTEXT.md medical safety rules and the evidence reviewed:

#### Recommended Language

| Instead of (diagnostic) | Use (comparative) |
|------------------------|-------------------|
| "The patient has right ramus hypoplasia" | "Right ramus height is X% smaller than left" |
| "Condylar asymmetry is present" | "A difference of X% was measured between right and left condylar height" |
| "Asymmetry is clinically significant" | "The measured difference exceeds the 6% technical error margin; clinical correlation is recommended" |
| "The patient requires surgery" | "3D imaging (CBCT) is recommended for further evaluation" |

#### Required Limitations to State

Every report MUST include:

1. **2D projection limitation**: "Measurements are derived from a 2D projection of 3D anatomy. Panoramic radiographs have inherent magnification and distortion that may affect measurement accuracy."

2. **Positioning sensitivity**: "Measurements are sensitive to patient head positioning during image acquisition. Head rotation may create apparent asymmetry."

3. **Landmark identification error**: "Measurements depend on manual landmark placement and are subject to inter-observer variability, particularly for condylion identification."

4. **No diagnostic conclusion**: "This is a measurement and comparative analysis tool, not a diagnostic system. Results must be interpreted in the context of clinical examination and adjunct imaging."

5. **Threshold caveat**: "The threshold values used are based on published literature and expert opinion, not on validated clinical outcomes. Apparent asymmetry may reflect technical factors rather than true anatomical asymmetry."

### When Should 3D Imaging (CBCT) Be Recommended?

The application should recommend CBCT when:

1. **Asymmetry index exceeds the 6% technical error threshold** — values above 6% (Habets index) or 12% (relative difference) are more likely to represent true anatomical asymmetry rather than technical artifact.

2. **Clinical signs are present** — the measured asymmetry is accompanied by clinical signs (facial asymmetry, deviated chin, occlusal cant, unilateral crossbite, TMJ symptoms).

3. **Progressive asymmetry** — serial measurements show increasing asymmetry over time (suggesting active condylar resorption or hemimandibular hyperplasia).

4. **Surgical planning is contemplated** — any case where orthognathic surgery or asymmetry correction is being considered requires 3D imaging.

5. **Discrepancy between OPG and clinical findings** — when the OPG shows minimal asymmetry but clinical examination reveals significant facial asymmetry (or vice versa), 3D imaging is necessary to resolve the discrepancy.

## 12. Unresolved Questions for OrthoBot

The following questions require OrthoBot's clinical expertise to resolve:

### Q1: Landmark Set — Should the sigmoid notch be included?
**Background:** The Habets method separates condylar (Co–Sn) and ramal (Sn–Go) height. This requires 3 bilateral landmarks (Co, Sn, Go) per side. However, sigmoid notch identification adds error, and the total height (Co–Go) captures the same asymmetry signal.

**Question:** Should the MVP use the full Habets decomposition (condylar + ramal + total) or only total height (Co–Go)?

### Q2: Body Length — Should mandibular body length be included in the MVP?
**Background:** Horizontal measurements on OPG are less reliable than vertical. Faryal & Shaikh (2022) found significant OPG vs cephalogram differences for body length (p=0.000). However, the PROJECT_CONTEXT.md lists "Mandibular body length measurement" as MVP scope item 5.

**Question:** Should body length be included with explicit reliability caveats, or excluded until CBCT-grade data is available?

### Q3: Body Length Landmarks — What reference point for the symphysis?
**Background:** If body length is included, the midline reference is either Menton (Me, most inferior point) or the symphysis midpoint. On OPG, menton may be easier to identify but represents the inferior border, not the true midline.

**Question:** Which midline landmark should be used for body length measurement?

### Q4: Threshold Communication — How should thresholds be presented?
**Background:** The 3% threshold is widely used but not validated. The 6% technical error margin is better supported. Using 3% as the sole threshold would flag 48–81% of normal patients as "asymmetric."

**Question:** Should the application use 3%, 6%, or a tiered system (normal / borderline / likely asymmetry)? What wording should accompany each tier?

### Q5: Calibration — Should the MVP support calibration or work with relative measurements only?
**Background:** For asymmetry indices, the calibration factor cancels in the ratio. Calibration is only needed if absolute measurements (in mm) are displayed. Laster et al. (2005) showed internal calibration is more accurate than using the manufacturer's stated magnification.

**Question:** Should the MVP display absolute measurements (requiring calibration) or only relative/percentage measurements (no calibration needed for asymmetry assessment)?

### Q6: Dominant Side Wording — How should the dominant side be communicated?
**Background:** The Habets formula produces a signed value (positive = right larger, negative = left larger). The clinical interpretation should state which side is larger/smaller without implying pathology.

**Question:** What exact wording should be used? (e.g., "Right ramus height is X% greater than left" vs "The right side is the dominant side")

### Q7: How should the Habets index and relative difference be labeled to avoid confusion?
**Background:** The relative difference formula produces values exactly twice the absolute Habets index. A 3% Habets index = 6% relative difference. Displaying both without clear labeling could confuse clinicians.

**Question:** Should the application display both values? If so, how should they be labeled to prevent misinterpretation?

### Q8: Should the application support the Kjellberg asymmetry index as an alternative?
**Background:** The Kjellberg index uses a different scale (100% = symmetric) and is less commonly used. Including it adds complexity but may be preferred by some clinicians.

**Question:** Is the Kjellberg index used in OrthoBot's clinical environment, or should only Habets/relative difference be included?
